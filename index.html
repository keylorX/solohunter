1The process, step by step

  1. High-pressure side. On the left the gas is squeezed into a small volume: molecules are crowded together, colliding constantly and moving fast. Fast molecules are exactly what a high temperature (T₁ = 300 K) means.
  2. Throttling. The only way out is through the narrow valve or throttle — in Joule and Thomson’s original 1852 experiment, a porous plug of compressed cotton wool. The gas is forced steadily through it.
  3. Expansion. Behind the restriction the gas spreads into a much larger volume at low pressure. Molecules drift apart — and as they separate, each one has to pull against the weak attractive forces its neighbours exert on it (real gases are not ideal!).
  4. Cooling. Pulling apart against those attractions costs energy. The pipe is insulated (Q = 0) and no engine does work, so the energy can only come from the molecules’ own kinetic energy. Slower molecules ⇒ lower temperature ⇒ T₂ ≈ 273 K. That is the observed cooling effect.

2Why does the gas cool? The molecular story

Picture the molecules as marbles connected to their neighbours by tiny weak springs (the van der Waals attractions). Far apart, the springs are relaxed. When the gas expands through the throttle, every molecule must move away from the others — stretching millions of tiny springs. Stretching a spring stores energy in it, and energy is conserved: the energy stored in the springs (potential energy) is taken from the speed of the molecules (kinetic energy).

Temperature is nothing but a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. Less kinetic energy ⇒ slower molecules ⇒ colder gas. The thermometer on the low-pressure side therefore reads lower, and with a big enough pressure drop the gas can even cool so much that mist forms or the pipe frosts over.

Key insight: for an ideal gas the Joule–Thomson effect is exactly zero — ideal-gas molecules exert no forces on each other, so there is nothing to “pay” for when they separate and the temperature would not change. The cooling you see is a signature of real-gas intermolecular forces.

3The thermodynamics in one look

The throttle is insulated and has no moving shaft, so for a steadily flowing stream: heat Q = 0, shaft work W = 0, and kinetic/potential-energy changes are negligible. The steady-flow energy balance then gives h₁ = h₂ — throttling is an isenthalpic (constant-enthalpy) process. The temperature change per unit pressure drop at constant enthalpy is the Joule–Thomson coefficient:

μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H   ⇒   dT = μJT dP  (dP < 0 through the valve)

If μJT > 0 the gas cools on expansion; if μJT < 0 it warms. The sign flips at the gas’s inversion temperature — below it, attractions dominate (cooling); above it, repulsions dominate (warming). Approximate values at room temperature:

Helium (He)≈ −0.06≈ 45warms up

Watch out: hydrogen and helium warm when throttled at room temperature because their inversion temperatures lie far below 300 K. That is why hydrogen must be pre-cooled (e.g. with liquid nitrogen) before it can be liquefied by Joule–Thomson expansion.

4Meet it in real life

  • Spray / aerosol cans feel cold after spraying — the propellant gas expands through the nozzle and cools.
  • The Linde–Hampson process repeats the throttling in a cycle until air is cold enough to condense — how industrial liquid nitrogen is made.
  • Refrigerators and air conditioners throttle liquid refrigerant through an expansion valve — the same idea (strengthened by phase change).
  • Natural-gas plants use Joule–Thomson expansion valves to chill and dehydrate gas; the valve can frost with ice or hydrates.

5About the numbers in the animation

The animation uses realistic textbook values, but the visuals are exaggerated so your eye can follow the physics:

  • Gas assumed: nitrogen (N₂), throttled from P₁ = 100 bar to P₂ = 1 bar with μJT ≈ +0.27 K/bar ⇒ T₁ = 300 K → T₂ ≈ 273 K (μJT actually varies with pressure and temperature, so this is an estimate).
  • Average molecular speed goes as √T: only 517 m/s → 493 m/s (≈ 5% slower) in reality — the animation slows molecules much more than that so the change is visible.
  • The real density ratio across the valve would be about 100 : 1; the animation shows only about 2.5 : 1, and the apparatus geometry is not to scale.
